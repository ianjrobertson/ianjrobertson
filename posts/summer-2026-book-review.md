#### Grit vs. Match Quality
Do some things come more naturally to us than others? Is something being difficult a good indicator that it's the wrong profression for you? How do you know when you're good at something? 

I think match quality is important, but it's also important to pick your career based on what jobs are available, what pays well, what is convenient for you and your family. One of the chapters pushed back on Angela Duckworths book Grit. I think there's more nuance in finding things that come naturally to us and working on problems that are inherently hard. I think it's more a matter of interest. Can you commit yourself to learning about this problem for 40 years. 

That's another point I liked is that while we ourselves are changing and elastic, the career we pick is likely going to shift dramatically over the course of an entire career, especially in a field like computer science. Just since when I started my undergrad to now, computers can talk. There is always new things to learn. Being employable is very related to being able to learn new complicated things quickly, and provide value with them. 

### Book Three: The Pragmatic Programmer by Andy Hunt and David Thomas
I loved this book. I liked that for the most part it focused on high level engineering principles instead of nitty gritty technical details. I think that makes what this book says valuable across a constantly shifting tech landscape, which is what the authors intended. 

#### Broken Windows
I was familar with the broken window theory, and I thought the sections the devote to it were super helpful. The decay of software and it's corresponding maintance cost is extremeley critical to good engineering. One of the indicators of good software is that it is flexible and easy to change, as requirements and contexts inevitably change. Broken windows state that the quality of code will rise to the standards of the current code. People, (myself included) rationalize a change, or commit to the current form of abstractions or habits in a codebase. All the time I look at how someone else has does something, and copy their strategy without spending time to improve. If you see something that doesn't work, or is bad practice, improve it proactively, or else your future code wills suffer. 

After reading this section, I immediatelly noticed places in a project I was working on that could be improved. Small improvements compound. I think this principle is even more true with agentic coding. The codebase becomes the first-class documentation that claude is going to refer to when it comes to architechture and building new features. Agentic coding can only rise to level of the existing code quality, and can very quickly go downhill. By fixing broken windows it provides better context for humans and agents about what to do next.
